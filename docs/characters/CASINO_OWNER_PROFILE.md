# Casino Owner Character Profile

Last updated: 2026-07-27

## Purpose

This file is the accepted character dossier for `casino_owner`. It records
Vesper's portrait direction, pure-villain role, casino temptation logic, contract
origin, first-run escape, second-run punishment, and final showcase reward rule.
The dossier may retain the omniscient Void truth, but the mandatory casino route
presents only an unnamed nonhuman creditor. An optional second-run external
route may reveal the Void identity after Vesper's punishment; later DLC may
continue that force's wider origin.

Runtime JS/data remains the source of truth for shipped behavior. This file owns
the accepted narrative direction until casino owner quests, dealer scenes,
showcase rewards, contract scenes, and second-run punishment logic are rewritten.

## Accepted Direction

Vesper is a pure villain.

Do not soften him with a tragic justification, secret kindness, possession,
misunderstanding, or noble intent. The player should dislike him more as they
learn what he has done.

His evil is not loud. It is elegant, contractual, and completely deliberate. He
does not need to threaten people with a blade. He shows them what they want,
calls the trap a choice, then writes down the cost after they are too desperate
to refuse.

Accepted route direction:

- Vesper treats human lives, debt, fate, and souls as wagerable assets.
- He is not a demon worshipper or Void fanatic.
- He is a middleman and system designer who found a way to commercialize
  contracts backed by an inhuman creditor.
- The casino is a machine for discovering what people will sell when hope runs
  out.
- First run: the player cannot truly punish him. Vesper escapes.
- Second run: achievement memory lets the player recognize and expose the current
  run's Loaded Dice, then use his own contract rules against him.
- Final punishment: he wagers his own soul or fate, loses, and is taken by the
  unnamed contract creditor. A separate optional external route may later reveal
  that force as Void without redeeming or re-centering Vesper.

## Portrait And Presence

Use `casino_owner.webp` as the accepted Vesper portrait.

The image works because it reads as:

- Elegant.
- Expensive.
- Controlled.
- Predatory without needing obvious monstrous traits.
- Someone who would smile while explaining why a victim agreed to lose.

Vesper should look like a man who knows the price of every person in the room.
The glass, wine, red-black clothing, and aristocratic posture support the current
tone better than the older female casino-owner portrait.

The previous female casino-owner image has been removed. Do not reintroduce it as
Vesper or as an automatic casino-cast character.

## Public Mask

Vesper presents himself as a fair host and seller of opportunity.

He does not say, "I destroy people." He says:

- People choose.
- Rules are visible.
- Everyone may stop before the final wager.
- A casino only reveals what someone wanted badly enough to risk.

This mask is important because it lets him weaponize consent. His favorite lie is
that a rigged situation becomes moral because a desperate person signed it.

Public claims:

- He never forces anyone to gamble.
- Every rule is stated somewhere.
- Debt is only the shadow of desire.
- Losing proves the wager was too large, not that the house was wrong.
- Saving others is also a form of greed when someone pays with themselves.

## Core Values

Vesper believes:

- Everyone has a price.
- Kindness is one of the easiest desires to exploit.
- Losers have no right to complain about the rules.

He does not hate the poor or worship the rich. He values people by what they can
be made to risk.

To him:

- A desperate parent is valuable.
- A proud fighter is valuable.
- A frightened healer is valuable.
- A hero who wants to save everyone is extremely valuable.

This is why the protagonist interests him. The protagonist has a powerful soul
and a visible tendency to interfere when someone is about to be destroyed.

## Signature Lines

Primary signature line:

> "我從不逼人下注。我只是把他們真正想要的東西放到桌上。"

Supporting lines:

> "輸的人，總是比較會談公平。"

> "公平？當然公平。每個人都有輸光的權利。"

Use these as tonal anchors for future dialogue. They should not be repeated so
often that they become noise. The primary line is the most complete expression of
his philosophy.

## Origin

Vesper was once a fallen financier, debt buyer, or ruined aristocratic contract
man.

He was not originally a gambler who got lucky. His old skill was debt:

- Bad loans.
- Collateral.
- Failed inheritances.
- War debts.
- Desperate signatures.
- The precise moment when a person has nothing left except something intangible.

This background matters because he understands contracts before he understands
the casino. Gambling is the stage. Debt is the engine.

## Blank Collateral Contract

Vesper discovers the Blank Collateral Contract through a failed debt purchase.

Accepted discovery chain:

1. As a young financier or debt buyer, Vesper buys a bundle of unrecoverable old
   debts.
2. One document in the bundle is strange: no named creditor, no fixed collateral,
   no stable repayment amount.
3. When someone writes a promise onto it, the contract can fill in a cost.
4. The cost is not limited to money. It can become memory, luck, lifespan, fate,
   or eventually soul.
5. Vesper first understands it as a perfect debt instrument: it lets people who
   cannot pay still keep paying.
6. He traces the document through the black market.
7. He learns that such contracts are not ordinary law. Their creditor line is
   nonhuman. Optional second-run external canon may later identify it as Void.
8. The black market is willing to sell dangerous things. Vesper is willing to
   build a system from them.

The public or trade name should be "Blank Collateral Contract." The mandatory
casino story does not name the deeper force. The later external truth may be
"Void contract."

Do not call it "Void mortgage contract" as a final term. That sounds too modern
and too direct. The layered terminology is better:

- Surface term: Blank Collateral Contract.
- Mandatory-route description: unnamed nonhuman creditor.
- Optional external truth: Void contract.
- Casino use: house contract, dark contract, or final wager contract depending
  on the scene.

## Black Market Boundary

The black market is not the final villain of Vesper's route.

The black market sold or moved the Blank Collateral Contract because it was rare,
dangerous, and valuable. It does not need to care what Vesper did with it after
the sale.

Accepted boundary:

- The black market does not have an endless supply of Blank Collateral Contracts.
- After Vesper obtains the contract, it becomes his system.
- The black market may be immoral, greedy, and indifferent, but it is not the
  casino's mastermind.
- Vesper is responsible for turning the contract into a soul-harvesting casino
  engine.

This keeps the black market gray-black and usable later, while Vesper remains the
pure villain to punish.

## Casino System

Vesper adapts the Blank Collateral Contract into casino rules.

He does not use one trap for everyone. He studies each person's desire:

- Someone who wants strength sees a display-case weapon.
- Someone who wants to save a loved one sees a rescue wager.
- Someone who needs money receives free chips and debt.
- Someone afraid of death is offered a survival contract.
- Someone proud is challenged to prove they can win fairly.
- Someone kind is encouraged to risk themselves for another person.

The casino does not only take money. Money is the entry layer. At deeper layers
the house takes debt, future options, memories, fate, and souls.

## Human And Soul Trade

Vesper's central crime is human and soul trade.

People ruined by the casino do not merely owe coin. At the bottom of the debt
ladder, they can become collateral. Their contracts, bodies, labor, memories,
fate, or souls may be transferred through black-market channels or the
contract's unnamed creditor rules.

He should not be written as someone who accidentally crossed a line. He knows the
line. He sells tickets to it.

## Casino Dealer

`casino_dealer` is the route's key witness and guide.

Accepted name and identity:

- Name: 洛恩 / Lorne. Scripts continue using runtime id `casino_dealer` so
  display-name ownership stays separate from scene logic.
- Gender and age direction: male, early forties.
- Portrait: reuse
  `src/assets/images/art/characters/portraits/casino_dealer.webp`.
- Visual anchor: dark-skinned, shaved head, old facial scar, black-and-gold coat,
  precise hands, cards in one hand and dice in the other. He looks practiced and
  self-possessed until collection begins.

Character construction:

- Origin: a traveling professional dealer who believed cheating a wealthy house
  was a victimless skill. Vesper caught him altering a settlement table,
  converted the loss into Blank Collateral debt, and forced him to operate the
  same kind of rigged games against desperate customers.
- Core fear: being collected before anyone knows which harms Vesper ordered and
  which Lorne chose to commit himself.
- Core desire: survive long enough to leave one honest account and stop another
  person from taking his place at the table.
- Values: technique, readable rules, and the stubborn belief that a game could be
  fair, even though he has spent years proving otherwise.
- Inner contradiction: he wants freedom and partial atonement, but every shift he
  works to stay alive creates another victim.
- Voice: polished table banter, exact descriptions of hands and odds, dry humor
  as a shield, and no jokes once collection begins. His thumb checks card edges
  or die weight before difficult answers.

Route boundary:

- He is slippery and survival-minded, not purely good.
- He has knowingly helped Vesper push people into games and carries guilt.
- First run: Vesper uses a rigged guest set to settle Lorne's remaining life. The
  protagonist interrupts the table anchor and saves him, but Vesper escapes.
  Lorne gives over one Loaded Die, admits he helped weight it, and points toward
  the contract source without asking forgiveness.
- Second run: achievement memory exposes the current run's abnormal die weight.
  Lorne marks the guest set before settlement and supports the host/guest rule
  reversal that makes Vesper his own collateral.
- True-ending state: Lorne opens the display case, turns over the debt ledger,
  and keeps only transparent ticket games running under town oversight while
  restitution is processed. This is work, not absolution or ownership of the
  casino as a reward.

The dealer should not replace Vesper as the villain. Lorne is compromised,
useful, and afraid. Vesper is the person who made the machine.

## Protagonist Hook

Vesper notices the protagonist for two reasons:

1. The protagonist saves `casino_dealer`, disrupting Vesper's collection process.
2. The protagonist shows interest in the display-case prizes.

This tells Vesper two things:

- The protagonist is willing to act for others.
- The protagonist still wants power, gear, or rare outcomes.

Vesper sees a high-value soul with exploitable kindness and visible desire. That
is enough for him to begin shaping the owner route around the protagonist.

The mandatory Chapter 4 escalation occurs after Gray Ridge. Vesper does not wait
for an optional casino visit. He intercepts the protagonist on the town return
and prices the current wound:

- First run: `失去一個人以後，金幣突然顯得很便宜。你若想讓下一次不同，展示櫃還在。`
- Second run: `把兩個人都帶回來，只會更清楚「差一點」值多少。你若想讓下一次也不同，展示櫃還在。`

Lorne refuses to open a private-collateral table that night. Vesper answers by
folding a new mark into Lorne's collateral page rather than punishing him in
public. This makes Vesper's personalized predation and Lorne's increasingly
costly resistance visible before the Chapter 5 settlement setup.

## First Run Route

The first run cannot truly punish Vesper.

Accepted first-run shape:

1. The player enters the casino through the normal temptation loop.
2. The player inspects display-case prizes and starts wanting the reward.
3. The player saves `casino_dealer` from a lethal consequence or collection
   event.
4. The dealer begins to expose the casino's deeper contract logic.
5. The player discovers that Vesper cheats through Loaded Dice and contract-
   backed house rules.
6. Vesper escapes before final punishment.
7. The dealer, no longer able to hide behind the house, quietly confesses part of
   their guilt and gives the player Vesper's Loaded Dice.
8. In the first run, the player understands the dice only as cheating evidence.

First-run player emotion:

- Hatred toward Vesper.
- Frustration that he escapes.
- Suspicion that the dice matter more than the route can yet explain.

## Loaded Dice

The Loaded Dice are the key object for Vesper's punishment.

They are physically weighted guest dice, not a spell that forces one exact face.
Their bias lowers guest odds without making every throw deterministic. The Blank
Collateral system simply treats the witnessed table result as binding; it does
not move the dice. On the first run, the player recognizes only cheating. In the
second run, achievement memory makes the same abnormal weight visible before the
settlement is finalized.

Accepted rules:

- Vesper uses the dice to tilt games toward the house.
- The dice help express that his "fairness" has always been false.
- In first run, the dice cannot punish Vesper because the player lacks the
  second-run understanding and Vesper has escaped.
- In second run, the player can use the dice against Vesper.
- The same current-run set gives Vesper the low odds he assigned to guests. He
  accepts those odds under his own witnessed wager rule and loses the fixed story
  throw without the player swapping, enchanting, or carrying dice across runs.

Dealer handoff:

- The dealer gives the dice to the player after Vesper escapes.
- The dealer does this while quietly admitting guilt.
- The dealer does not ask for forgiveness.
- The dealer knows the dice prove cheating, but not necessarily the full
  second-run counteruse.

Suggested emotional line direction for the dealer:

> "我知道它不夠。它什麼都還不了。但如果你還想追他，拿著這個。他從來沒有靠運氣贏過。"

## Second Run Punishment

The second run gives the player the missing understanding.

Accepted second-run shape:

1. The `莊家離席` achievement preserves the player's memory of the die's abnormal
   weight; no physical die crosses the reset.
2. The player identifies the current run's loaded guest set before settlement.
   The dice are house bias made physical.
3. The player saves or turns `casino_dealer` early enough to expose the contract
   path.
4. The dealer marks the current-run set in front of the witness without replacing
   any die.
5. The player invokes Vesper's written dispute rule: refuse the same-set seat
   exchange and all private collateral becomes void under civic audit, or accept
   the guest position and preserve the house claim if he wins.
6. Vesper knowingly accepts the lower guest odds because surrendering would
   immediately cost him the collateral pages, owner seat, and ledger. He believes
   one throw is a tolerable risk and repeats his own trap: `我只需要贏一次。`
7. Equivalent collateral requires his ownership and personal fate on the page.
   He confirms both in writing before the throw.
8. The marked weighted set produces the same disadvantage imposed on victims and
   stops on a losing result.
9. Vesper first refuses to believe he lost.
10. He accuses the player of cheating.
11. The active page is already the master settlement contract. Tearing it counts
    as withdrawal and immediate loss under the anti-escape clauses he wrote for
    victims.
12. He begs the contract's creditor not to collect him.
13. The contract executes anyway.
14. The unnamed creditor takes Vesper. A separate optional external route may
    later identify it as Void.
15. The player may choose one display-case grand prize.

The emotional target is satisfaction. This is not a tragic ending for Vesper. It
is a predator being eaten by the system he built.

## Final Wager

The final game is fixed as one witnessed three-die settlement throw at the main
contract table. It is not roulette, cards, a player-selected minigame, or a
combat substitute.

Vesper is not fooled about the dice. He knows the guest set is weighted and that
losing is possible. His own temptation is control: he prefers a bad chance to the
certain loss of the house, ledger, and collateral pages. This completes his
philosophy rather than making him suddenly stupid. The weighted roll remains
chance under bad odds; the contract only enforces the accepted outcome.

## Collapse

Vesper's final collapse should happen in stages:

1. He does not believe the losing result applies to him.
2. He tries to keep his elegant composure.
3. He accuses the protagonist of cheating.
4. He searches for a loophole.
5. He begs the creditor or the contract to spare him.
6. He is collected anyway.

This makes his hypocrisy visible. He built a life on "willing wagers" and
"fairness," then cannot accept either when he becomes the collateral.

## Contract Collection And External Void Truth

The mandatory casino route should show the contract's unnamed creditor taking
Vesper as execution, not random punishment. The optional second-run external
story may identify the force as Void later, but casino dialogue, UI, enemies,
items, and affinities do not.

Detailed visual staging can be decided later. Current accepted direction:

- No civic trial is needed.
- No redemptive speech is needed.
- No heroic execution is needed.
- The contract simply recognizes him as the losing collateral.
- The creditor collects what he wagered.

This should feel clean, horrifying, and deserved.

## Showcase Reward

After Vesper's second-run punishment, the player may choose one display-case
grand prize.

Rules:

- The reward should be a concrete visible showcase item.
- The selected reward should match its display-case art and mechanical item.
- Do not reward a vague box unless the chosen object is explicitly a box.
- The choice should feel like taking back what the casino used as temptation.
