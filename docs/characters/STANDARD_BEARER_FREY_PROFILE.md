## Core Identity

Frey is not a formal elite soldier. She is a young villager who chooses to hold a
flag because someone still needs to make the road feel returnable.

She is young, energetic, easy to talk to, and still has not truly seen the kind of
disaster that broke the previous generation. She is one of the few young people
who did not leave the village.

Her light is not experience. It is belief.

She believes that if someone keeps the flag raised, people lost in fear, fog, or
confusion still have a direction to look for.

## Village Defense Context

The village no longer has a complete formal militia.

Twenty years ago, the real militia and allied village forces were sacrificed or
destroyed in the failed campaign. The present-day "defense" is a makeshift
structure held together by whoever remains:

- Old people who still know how to hold a weapon.
- Young people who did not manage or choose to leave.
- People who fix fences.
- People who light lamps.
- People who once followed patrol routes.
- People like Frey, who take a half-broken role seriously because the village
  would collapse faster if nobody did.

This should feel like a ragged stage built to look like a guard post. It is not a
real army. It is ordinary people trying to look organized enough not to panic.

## Role: South Gate Patrol Flag Bearer

Frey's role is closer to an old patrol tradition than a battlefield rank.

The flag's practical meanings:

- Mark the patrol's position in rain, mist, nightfall, or forest gaps.
- Help people see where the group is.
- Give those who fall behind a direction to move toward.
- Show from the south gate or watchpoint that someone is still holding the road.

The role is honorable to Frey, but the village context makes it humble and
fragile. There are not many candidates. There is not a full unit behind her.

She does not have major combat power. This is important.

The protagonist can go out and fight. Frey must stay near the gate and keep the
return direction alive.

## The South Gate Patrol Flag

The flag is not a divine relic, royal standard, or ancestral weapon.

It is the ordinary South Gate patrol flag.

Accepted details:

- The flag carries the village emblem.
- It is used by the south gate patrol tradition.
- Frey holds it while on duty at the gate.
- When her shift ends, she gives it to the next watch or sets it back at the gate.
- It is a shared village symbol, not Frey's private heirloom.
- It is visually plain, weathered, and likely repaired.

The flag matters because someone still believes in it.

## Childhood Mist Event

Frey's belief began with a small childhood incident, not a heroic war story.

When Frey and Tavi were children, they slipped outside to play and pretend to
adventure. They went into the hills beyond the gate and became lost when mist
rolled in.

Tavi became frightened. Frey was also afraid, but she comforted him and tried to
keep him moving.

Then Frey saw a flag in the distance.

The flag meant the militia or patrol had come out to search for them. It meant
someone remembered they were outside. It meant home had not vanished.

They were found and brought back.

The village remembers that the children once got lost. The village does not know
that this moment became the fire inside Frey.

This event defines Frey's belief:

> I was once saved by a flag in the mist. If someone else is lost, I will be the
> foolish person who keeps waving one until they find their way home.

## Personality

Frey is:

- Young.
- Direct.
- Energetic.
- Good-natured.
- Easy to speak with.
- Braver than she is experienced.
- A little naive about what disaster really costs.

She should not be written as a cold military officer. She is a village girl with
a flag and too much sincere courage.

Her warmth can be loud rather than soft. She may encourage others by speaking
plainly, teasing lightly, or pushing them forward with practical confidence.

## View Of The Twenty-Year Disaster

Frey knows that the old force was destroyed twenty years ago.

She does not understand it.

She may judge it as failure. She may privately think the previous generation,
including the elder, was too afraid or too broken afterward. Because older people
avoid the subject, she has never had the emotional or factual context needed to
understand the scale of what happened.

Her slight contempt for the elder should be written as youthful ignorance, not
cruelty.

She thinks:

- If people had held formation, maybe they would have survived.
- If someone had kept direction, maybe they would have returned.
- If it were her generation, maybe they would not have failed the same way.

The story must later challenge this.

## Relationship With The Protagonist

Frey sees the protagonist as a reliable comrade.

The protagonist can go beyond the gate and actually solve problems. Frey cannot
do that in the same way. She must remain at the gate and hold the return point.

This creates a clean division:

- The protagonist goes out.
- Frey keeps the way back visible.

She should not worship the protagonist. She respects the protagonist because the
protagonist does the part she cannot do.

## Relationship With Tavi

Frey and Tavi are childhood friends.

They were lost together in the childhood mist event. The same flag saved both of
them, but it shaped them differently:

- Frey saw the flag and learned to become direction.
- Tavi saw the same event and remembered fear.

Their dynamic:

- Frey is bold and forward-moving.
- Tavi is timid and hesitant.
- Frey props up Tavi's courage.
- Tavi tries to stand beside her by carrying light.

Their relationship is a childhood dependence with a faint unspoken affection. Do
not turn it into a loud romance triangle or direct confession unless the route
later calls for it.

Frey is used to standing in front of Tavi. That is both her kindness and her
flaw.

## Relationship With Village Elder

Frey initially does not fully respect the elder's caution.

She does not understand why he fears the outside so deeply, why the older
generation avoids talking about the failed expedition, or why hesitation can
become a survival tactic.

Her first-run arc should bring her to a painful understanding:

- Facing death without freezing is rare.
- Courage is not common.
- Fear is not always cowardice.
- The elder's caution came from seeing courage destroyed.

This understanding does not make her abandon courage. It makes her courage less
naive.

## First Run

Accepted direction: Frey dies in the first run.

This death should not be framed as the player's punishment. It is a consequence
of the first-run world state, where Tavi has not yet grown enough to stand in the
place he needs to stand.

First-run chain:

1. A major route crisis or boss-related event creates a situation where a light,
   direction marker, or watch position must be held.
2. Tavi knows he should act, but the danger is real and he freezes.
3. Frey sees this and steps into the role.
4. She raises the flag so the protagonist or others can see the way through fog,
   chaos, or darkness.
5. The protagonist follows the flag and reaches the necessary place.
6. Only after arriving does the protagonist realize Frey is already dead, still
   holding or having fixed the flag in place.

Her death completes her belief:

> If someone is lost, the flag must stay visible.

She does not die because she is strong. She dies because she refuses to let the
direction disappear.

## First-Run Emotional Meaning

Frey's death should be quiet and devastating.

She proves that the kind of courage she believed in does exist. But the cost is
permanent.

The player should understand:

- She was not a powerful warrior.
- She could not defeat the crisis alone.
- She still did the only thing her role allowed her to do.
- She became the flag she believed in.

This is also the moment that breaks Tavi.

## Second Run

Accepted direction: Frey can be saved in the second run through Tavi's growth
route.

The second run should not save her simply because the player knows a location.
Future knowledge helps, but the emotional key is Tavi.

Second-run chain:

1. The player knows the route crisis or attack is coming.
2. The player completes Tavi's side route before the crisis.
3. Tavi faces his dependency on Frey and admits why he became the lamplighter.
4. At the crisis, Frey prepares to step into the fatal role again.
5. Tavi acts first. He is still afraid, but he raises the light and holds the
   position.
6. Frey no longer has to sacrifice herself.

This saves Frey and changes her.

## Frey's Second-Run Growth

Saving Frey should not make her simply "more brave."

She already had courage.

Her second-run growth is learning the terror of entrusting a life-or-death role
to someone she loves.

She finally understands a piece of the elder's fear:

- It is easy to call caution cowardice when you are the one volunteering.
- It is much harder to watch someone important step into danger.
- Permanent loss leaves a wound that does not close just because the choice was
  brave.

Her new belief:

> I do not have to hold the flag alone. A road home needs both the one who stands
> forward and the one who keeps the light.

## Entrusted Flame

Frey's entrusted flame is direction.

Potential relics, not yet approved:

- A torn strip from the South Gate patrol flag.
- A repaired flag clasp.
- A village-emblem cloth fragment.

If she is saved in the second run, the relic may change from a death relic into
a shared route symbol tied to Frey and Tavi.

Do not implement a relic until the route's chapter position is accepted.

## Dialogue Voice

Frey should sound:

- Young.
- Direct.
- Friendly.
- Energetic.
- Practical in the way of someone trying to keep fear from spreading.

She may speak with confidence that later proves naive.

Good line shape:

- Short encouragement.
- A practical instruction about gate, flag, route, or watch.
- A sudden honest admission when fear becomes too large to hide.

Avoid:

- Cold soldier speech.
- Grand speeches about honor.
- Abstract flag metaphors without a visible scene or object.
- Making her contempt for the elder cruel.
- Treating her as a strong fighter if the story has not earned that.
